01

Overview

A five-minute form that takes five weeks to trust

A learner’s licence is many Indians’ first contact with a government service as an adult. The Parivahan → Sarathi journey is technically online, but in practice it is completed with a parent on speakerphone, a friend cropping a photo, and a driving-school agent on WhatsApp. This case study starts from that gap between “available online” and “completable alone” — and designs the missing layer: guidance, proof and plain language.

The problem

Independence breaks at the edges

Aadhaar login works. The details form is fine. People fall out of the journey at the unglamorous edges: an unexplained photo rule, a wrong RTO, a status label that reads like a verdict.

The bet

Clarity is the feature

Not a new portal, not more content. Show requirements before they bite, recommend the RTO from the PIN code, explain every status with an owner and a next action, and make payment proof durable.

The build

A working, testable prototype

Research was translated into wireframes and then a responsive 32-screen prototype — saved progress, realistic edge cases and failure recovery — because a case study that stops at principles can’t be tested.

02

Context & problem

High stakes, low guidance, no second attempt at a first impression

Applying for a learner’s licence is a common, time-bound, money-bound service that most people use exactly once or twice in a lifetime. That makes it a poor candidate for “users will learn the interface”: there is no learning curve available, only drop-off, a wasted trip, or a dependency formed for life.

Primary problem statement

First-time applicants struggle to complete the application independently because of unclear guidance, poor status communication, confusing RTO selection and low system trust — resulting in anxiety and heavy reliance on external help.

Everything below is a supporting failure of that one sentence, not a separate project.

Six supporting problems

  • Users cannot confidently identify and trust the official portal.
  • Photo requirements and error messages are poorly communicated.
  • RTO selection does not match how people think about location.
  • Statuses do not explain meaning, owner or next action.
  • Payment flows create doubt about money and saved data.
  • The experience violates a first-timer’s mental model of “online”.

Who this is for, and what was in scope

Why this problem is worth designers’ time.

The failures are not exotic. They are labels, sizes, defaults and confirmation screens. They are cheap to fix and unusually expensive to ignore: a wrong RTO costs a day’s leave and a 40 km round trip; a rejected photo costs a week of waiting; a payment that “may have failed” costs trust in every future government transaction.

Four journeys, one repeated story

Qualitative interviews focused on what people actually did — where they searched, what they screenshotted, whom they called — rather than what they thought of the interface. Observations were clustered into an affinity board, then reduced to themes with evidence attached.

Research limitation, stated plainly.

Four interviews in two locations, conducted as a hackathon sprint. The sample skews young, urban and English-literate. 04

Synthesis

Seven themes that account for almost every drop-off

Each theme survived clustering because it appeared across more than one interview and could be traced to a specific screen or message. Severity reflects the real-world cost of the failure, not how loudly it was described.

01

Discovery is a trust crisis

Search results are crowded with ads and look-alike agents. The Parivahan → Sarathi redirect wipes the visual identity users had just verified, so they arrive unsure whether they are on the government site at all.

Evidence: Ajay worked through several search results and look-alike sites before reaching the official one. P1

02

Photo upload is a hidden exam

Dimension, size and background rules exist but appear only as a rejection. Errors don’t say which rule failed, so people leave the portal to find a tool that will “fix” the file.

Evidence: Rohan’s photo was rejected, then he left the portal for a third-party resizer. P0

03

RTO selection asks the wrong question

People choose by city or by the name they recognise. Jurisdiction is decided by address and PIN code, which the form never connects to the choice — so the error surfaces days later, at the centre.

Evidence: Riya booked the wrong RTO and waited two hours at the centre. P0

04

“Under Scrutiny” is a dead end

A status that names an internal process, with no owner, reason or next action. People cannot tell whether they made a mistake, whether they must act, or whether waiting is correct.

Evidence: Tamanna refreshed repeatedly, unsure whether to wait, visit the RTO or involve an agent. P0

05

Payment anxiety outlives the payment

Session timeouts and vague failures make people fear both losing money and losing data. Screenshotting a receipt becomes a workaround for a missing confirmation model.

Evidence: Rohan kept a screenshot but could not say whether he had been charged. P1

06

The mental model is “fully online”

Aadhaar plus a website reads as “no office visit”. The product never corrects that expectation early, so the eventual physical step feels like a broken promise rather than a planned milestone.

Evidence: Riya and Rohan both assumed the test would be online-only. P1

07

Help is a symptom, not a feature

Every workaround — family, friends, Google, resizing tools, agents — is a self-service gap that has been outsourced to a human. Dependency is the outcome the interface is quietly designing for.

Evidence: All four participants used at least one external helper. Cross-cutting

The synthesis, in one line

The portal explains the process to itself, not to a person doing it for the first time.

That reframing is what turned a list of complaints into a design brief: every screen must answer where am I, what is this, and what happens next?

05

Journey mapping

Confidence peaks at login, then falls off a cliff

Plotting emotional confidence against the nine stages of the current journey exposes the real shape of the problem: the portal earns trust during Aadhaar verification, then spends it on upload rules, jurisdiction and an unreadable status label. Relief arrives only when someone else takes over.

Moment 1 “Your photo was rejected.” The first hard stop. No rule named, no example shown, no way to fix it in the portal — the applicant leaves and may not return on the same device.
Moment 2 “Which RTO do I pick?” A city list that looks like a location question but is really a jurisdiction question. The cost of a wrong answer is hidden until the appointment.
Moment 3 “Under Scrutiny.” Two words, no owner, no date, no action. The user cannot distinguish “you made a mistake” from “we are working on it”.

The current journey, end to end

As captured in interviews
  1. Search Google, dodge ads and unofficial agents.
  2. Land on Parivahan, get redirected to Sarathi.
  3. Sign up or log in with Aadhaar and OTP.
  4. Fill personal details — comparatively smooth.
  5. Upload photo and documents — high friction.
  6. Pay the fee — high anxiety, unclear failure.
  7. Select RTO and appointment — often wrong.
  8. Track status — meanings unclear.
  9. Liveness check at the RTO — more friction.
  10. Ask family, friends, Google or an agent.

How might we…

Eight questions carried into design

Discovery

…help users find and trust the official portal in one search?

Answer with a verified-domain marker and one continuous visual identity.

Photo upload

…help users submit an acceptable photo on the first attempt?

Answer with requirements before the picker, then live, per-rule validation.

RTO selection

…guide users to the correct RTO by jurisdiction, not city name?

Answer with a PIN-based recommendation, a map, and a confirmation moment.

Branding

…reduce confusion when Parivahan hands over to Sarathi?

Answer with one continuous identity and a verified-official marker that persists across the handover.

Status

…make every status explain itself and name the next action?

Answer with a four-part status: what, why, who, by when.

Progress

…give users a constant sense of where they are and what is left?

Answer with a persistent progress strip, repeated in words: “2 of 5 steps done”.

Payment

…eliminate payment anxiety and provide lasting proof?

Answer with a strong success state, receipt access and clear failure semantics.

Independence

…let a first-timer finish without family, friends or agents?

Answer by designing self-service recovery instead of a helpline as the fallback.

08

Design principles

Six rules that settled arguments

Principles are only useful if they can veto a decision. These were written to be specific enough to point at a screen and say “that breaks rule four”.

Clarity over cleverness

Plain words, one idea per screen, no internal vocabulary. “Under Scrutiny” becomes “We’re checking your address proof”. If a label needs a glossary, it is the wrong label.

Prevent errors before they happen

Requirements appear before the file picker. The RTO is recommended from the address already entered. Confirmation is a deliberate screen, not a toast.

Make trust visible

Official identity is continuous from search to receipt. Every irreversible action — payment, submission, slot booking — produces evidence the user can keep.

Match the mental model

Say early what happens online and what happens at the RTO. The physical step is a planned milestone, not a surprise. Progress is expressed in the user’s words, not the system’s.

Design for independence

Every screen must be completable alone on a phone. If a step needs a different file, a bigger screen or a human, the interface must say so before the user is stuck.

Always show the next step

No dead ends and no ambiguous states. Every status, error and confirmation ends with an action, a date, or an explicit “nothing to do”.

09

Low-fidelity wireframes

Three P0 flows, sketched before pixels

Wireframes were used to test the structure of the reassurance, not the layout: where the explanation sits, what the error says, and what the user can do next. All three flows keep a single action per screen and put the reason beside the decision.

What changed between sketch and build

  • RTO moved after the address step, so the PIN code is already known.
  • Photo checks became a checklist that fills in live, not a single pass/fail.
  • “Under Scrutiny” was retired as a user-facing phrase; it survives only as an internal note in the timeline.
  • Every flow gained an exit: change, fix, or get help with this step.

Constraint checks

  • No step depends on a file the applicant can’t produce on a phone.
  • Every recommendation is explainable in one sentence (“covers your PIN code”).
  • Failure states model real causes seen in interviews: size, dimensions, jurisdiction, session timeout.
  • No dark patterns: no urgency timers, no pre-ticked consents, no forced agent hand-off.

The same screen would say one of exactly two things:

No money has left your account. Try again — your application and documents are saved.

  1. Success is a screen, not a toast. It confirms the amount, the application number and the transaction in one place, so nothing needs to be reconstructed from memory.

  3. Failure has two semantics. “No money left” and “money debited, slot unconfirmed” are different situations with different instructions — never one generic “Payment Failed”.
  4. The data is safe by design. The failure message explicitly says the application and documents are saved, which removes the fear of starting over.

Voice rules

  • Sentence case, second person, no government code names.
  • Lead with what is happening, then what the user must do.
  • Quantify: amounts, file sizes, dates, step counts.
  • Never blame the user; describe the file, not the person.
  • Every message ends in an action, a date, or “nothing to do”.

Accessibility

Built in, not audited on

A service used once cannot rely on learned workarounds. Accessibility decisions were made while building each screen, and are documented here so they can be verified rather than assumed.

  • ✓Landmarks and headings. One h1 per screen, ordered headings, skip link to main content, labelled navigation.
  • ✓Visible focus. A high-contrast amber ring with offset on every interactive element; never removed.
  • ✓Status announcements. Error and status messages use role="alert" or polite live regions, so progress is heard, not just seen.
  • ✓Target size. 2.9rem minimum for controls, including icon-only buttons and slot chips.
  • ✓Labels and hints. Every field is programmatically labelled; hints are linked, not placeholder-only.
  • ✓No colour-only meaning. Status pairs colour with an icon and a sentence; progress repeats as text.
  • …Contrast. Body and UI pairs target 4.5:1 / 3:1; a full automated contrast pass is still pending.
  • …Assistive-tech testing. Structural work is done; moderated testing with screen-reader and low-vision users is the next phase.

Also designed for

  • Small screens. 375 px layouts, stacked actions, no horizontal scroll except the deliberate journey chart.
  • Zoom and reflow. Built to reflow at 320 px and 150% zoom without loss of content; still to be verified in testing.
  • Reduced motion. Transitions and smooth scrolling disabled under prefers-reduced-motion.
  • Offline and interruption. A polite banner reassures users that saved progress is safe when connectivity drops.
  • Print. This case study and the prototype’s receipt are both print-styled for people who keep paper.
What is not yet proven.

No formal WCAG audit has been run, and no assistive-technology user has tested the prototype. Claims here describe implemented intent. The next phase should include an axe/Lighthouse pass, manual keyboard-only walkthroughs, and testing with NVDA or VoiceOver.

Usability test plan

  • Warm-up: last time you needed a government service on your phone.
  • Task 1: find the official learner’s licence service and start an application.
  • Task 2: upload a photo — given a file that is deliberately 412 KB.
  • Task 3: confirm the RTO for an unfamiliar PIN code.
  • Task 4: explain what “we’re checking your address proof” means and whether any action is needed.
  • Task 5: pay, then find the receipt after navigating away.
  • Measures: completion without help, time on task, SEQ, comprehension answers, recovery behaviour.

Before / after plan

  • Baseline: code existing support contacts and RTO correction logs; intercept survey at two RTOs.
  • Prototype round: moderated, task-based, 5–8 participants, think-aloud.
  • Iteration: fix comprehension failures, re-test the three P0 flows.
  • Pilot: staged release with event instrumentation for the seven metrics above.
  • Guardrails: no drop in completion for low-literacy or regional-language users; no increase in duplicate payments.
Why no numbers are claimed yet. The prototype is a design artefact with synthetic data; it cannot generate a valid baseline. Reporting invented percentages would make this case study less useful, not more persuasive.
14

Reflection

The problems were never technical

Nothing in this redesign requires new policy or a new backend. It requires the interface to say what it means, show its evidence, and end every screen with an answer. That is an unusually cheap fix for an unusually expensive failure.

What I learned

  • Clarity is the product. The biggest blockers were labels, defaults and confirmation, not missing features.
  • Mental models are contracts. “Aadhaar means online” is a reasonable inference; the product breaks it silently and then punishes the user for believing it.
  • Small moments carry the emotion. One error string, one status label and one receipt decide whether the service feels trustworthy.
  • Dependency is designed. Every time the interface refuses to explain, it recruits a human into the process.
  • Government services must over-communicate. In a once-in-a-lifetime journey, there is no second visit to learn the interface.

What I’d do differently

  • Recruit for exclusion. The sample was young, urban and English-literate — exactly the users least likely to be blocked.
  • Test earlier and rougher. Sketches became full screens before anyone outside the project reacted to them.
  • Bring support staff in. Call-centre language would have exposed the real status vocabulary faster than interviews did.
  • Design the empty and slow cases. What happens when verification takes nine days, not three?
  • Start the content guide on day one. Retrofitting voice rules across 32 screens was the slowest part of the build.

Closing

The learner’s licence journey is usable in theory and stressful in practice. Fixing the RTO default, the photo rules, the status vocabulary, the payment proof and the progress model moves it from “I need help to finish this” to “I can do this myself, with confidence.”

The next chapter is not another screen. It is five to eight first-time applicants, a phone, a deliberately oversized photo — and a willingness to be wrong.
